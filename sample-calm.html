Chapter 1: "My mind won't stop at night."

The problem

The moment your head touches the pillow, the thoughts start. Lists, worries, "what ifs." You try to stop thinking, which only makes you think more.

Why it happens

During the day you're busy, so worries wait in line. At night, with no distractions, they all arrive at once. Your brain is trying to help: it thinks that if it keeps the problems in view, you won't forget them. Telling it to "stop" doesn't work, because it doesn't believe the problems are handled.

The fix: the bedtime to-do list and worry time

Part 1: the bedtime to-do list (5 minutes, just before you turn off the light).

Take paper. Write down everything you need to do in the next few days, as specifically as you can ("email Sam about the invoice", not "work stuff"). Add any worries, and next to each one write one tiny next step, or "nothing to do tonight." In a Baylor University sleep-lab study, people who spent 5 minutes writing a specific to-do list at bedtime fell asleep faster than people who wrote about what they had already done, and the more specific the list, the faster they fell asleep.

Part 2: scheduled worry time (15 minutes, every day, not near bedtime).

Pick a fixed time, for example 6:00 pm. During the day or night, when a worry shows up, tell yourself: "Not now. I'll think about this at 6." Write it down if needed. At 6:00, sit down and worry on purpose for 15 minutes. Then stop.

Practitioners use this technique to help people feel less controlled by worry. It feels strange at first. It gets easier with practice.

Try it now

Choose your worry time and set a daily phone reminder. Tonight, just before you turn off the light, write a 5-minute specific to-do list.

Check yourself

  • ☐ I have a fixed daily worry time that isn't near bedtime.
  • ☐ I wrote a specific to-do list at bedtime.
  • ☐ When a worry came early, I postponed it to worry time.
